What this is, and what it is not
  • Row one is the ordinary number. Sea level, sun's centre 50 minutes of arc below the geometric horizon, which is 16 for the sun's own radius and 34 for refraction. This is what a printed luach gives and what every zmanim library gives by default.
  • Row two adds your height. Standing higher lets you see over the curve of the earth, so the sun sets later. This is the same adjustment KosherJava makes when a location carries an elevation.
  • Row three is the one you asked for. It walks the ground outward along every bearing the sun could set on, allows for the curve of the earth and for refraction along that ray, and takes the highest thing in the way. The sun disappears behind that, not behind the sea.
  • Whether any of this should move a zman is a question for a posek, not for a calculator. Whether shkia follows the visible horizon or mishor is a real machlokes, and almost every printed luach uses sea level. This tool shows you the sky; it does not tell you which one counts.
  • The air is the limit, not the map. The 34 minutes of arc of refraction is a convention: real temperature and pressure move sunset by minutes, which is more than most terrain corrections. The elevation map is bare earth at roughly 30 to 90 metres, so trees, buildings and anything close to you are not in it, and a near obstruction is exactly what it resolves worst.
  • The spot that blocks the sun is usually closer than you expect, and that is the earth curving away rather than a mistake. The drop grows as the square of the distance: under a metre at 3 km, 60 m at 30 km, 242 m at 60 km. So from Lakewood a 44 m rise 3 km out stands 14 m above eye level, while a 65 m hill 30 km out is already 24 m below it. To beat that near rise, ground 30 km away would have to reach 220 m, and at 60 km it would need 533 m. The whole state tops out at 550 m, 130 km off. Checked at three different map resolutions, in case the far ground was simply being sampled too coarsely to show its peaks: the answer did not move.
  • Skyline here means the outline of the ground around you, bearing by bearing: for each compass direction, how high the highest land in that direction stands above or below your own eye. Zero is level with you. Three degrees up is a hill the sun has to set into; half a degree down is open ground you can see over. It is the same thing you would trace with your finger along the horizon if you turned slowly on the spot.
